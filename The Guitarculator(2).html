The two underlying terms — a stretch term (a12²·E·S / T·L) and a bending-stiffness term (2√(E·I/T)), with S=(π/4)d² and I=(π/64)d⁴ — come from Sjaak Elmendorp's published equations in "It's All About the Core, or How to Estimate Compensation," American Lutherie #104 (2010), freely available via the Guild of American Luthiers (luth.org). How the two terms combine, and core diameter, were reverse-engineered against Mottola's real calculator, not published anywhere. Elmendorp's own article adds the two terms directly, matching his own worked example almost exactly. Optimized (Price)'s Nut Comp. column looks unrelated to the other two on purpose, not by mistake: Simple and Advanced both work by splitting one shared total (a fraction of it goes to the nut, the rest to the saddle), so their nut numbers scale up and down with each string the same way the saddle numbers do. How the Advanced nut/saddle split works

Trevor Gore's book (Contemporary Acoustic Guitar Design and Build) describes a full numerical optimizer that jointly solves for nut and saddle compensation, fret by fret, from first principles (how a string's path bends around a fretting finger, the resulting tension change, and so on — Sec. 4.7.3, Equ. 4.7-23 through 4.7-37). This app tried, twice, to reimplement that literally from the book's equations and diagrams, and checked the result against three independent sources: the book's own text, the actual figures (4.7-15/4.7-16, transcribed from photographed pages), and a third-party builder's published reference spreadsheet that implements the same equations. In every case, some piece of the reconstruction came out wrong — the nut and saddle terms weren't cleanly separable the way the equations were being interpreted, so the optimizer either zeroed out nut compensation or landed on an arbitrary, unstable split, even when fed the reference spreadsheet's own exact numbers. That's documented in detail if you want it, but the short version: this specific piece of his method is genuinely hard to get right from the book alone, and rather than ship an unverified per-fret model, this app does something more modest and better-grounded instead.

What Advanced mode actually does now

It uses the RESULT of Gore's own optimizer directly. His book's Build volume (Table 21-2) publishes the real nut/saddle split his software produced for a real steel-string guitar, all 6 strings. What fraction of each string's total compensation (the same already-validated Simple-mode number) went to the nut versus the saddle turns out to fit a clean line against core diameter, fitted separately for wound and plain strings:

  • Plain strings: nut fraction ≈ 71.8% − 14.13 × core diameter (inches)
  • Wound strings: nut fraction ≈ 61.0% − 14.69 × core diameter (inches)

In practice: a thin plain string puts more than half its total compensation on the nut; a thick wound string puts roughly two-thirds on the saddle — both real, book-sourced patterns that a flat 50/50 split misses. Finger depression (the input Gore's own modelling found to be the single biggest driver of extra low-fret sharpness) nudges that fraction a little further toward the nut when set above his own stated "realistic" value (~0.3mm) and a little away from it when set below. Nut height, neck relief, and playing style are still shown here for reference and because they matter for other parts of this app, but they no longer independently drive this particular calculation.

Honest limits

This is a fit to one real published example (6 strings, one guitar, one set-up), not a from-scratch physical model — treat it as a better-than-50/50 approximation, not a per-setup optimum. It won't move if you change scale length, action, or relief the way a true per-fret model would. Simple mode's 50/50 split remains the fallback if you'd rather reason about a plainer, fully from-first-principles number.

Old Guitar Mode + Nut Compensation

Gore's book covers a second scenario directly (Fig. 4.7-5: "an example of radical nut compensation required to bring this old, left-handed Gibson into tune") — an existing instrument where you're not touching the bridge, so the saddle position is fixed at whatever it already measures. Turning on both Old Guitar Mode and Nut Compensation switches this app into that mode: the saddle is pinned to the real nut-to-saddle distance you enter, and both Simple and Advanced solve only for how much a new or re-cut nut needs to correct given that constraint, instead of freely optimizing both.

In development — third attempt, parked (not shipped)

A later session tried a third approach, suggested by a friend of the app's author: instead of reimplementing Gore's literal path-bending equations, build (or measure) a per-fret deflection lookup table — how far the string is pushed down at each fret, from the neck's real curvature plus finger pressure — then solve for whatever nut/saddle split best cancels the resulting per-fret tuning error across every fret at once, using ordinary 2-parameter least squares. The deflection side of this already exists in the code (buildActionModel(), computeFretErrorCents(), deltaCentsFromComp() — present but currently unused, look for them near the two earlier attempts). A position-dependent correction was also added to the small-strain stretch formula (stretch(x,a) = a²·E·S·L / (4·T·x·(L−x))), chosen so it reduces exactly to the already-validated saddle-only formula at fret 12 (x=L/2) while still varying correctly at other frets.

Tested against a representative 6-string steel-string set (D'Addario EJ16-equivalent gauges, typical action), the fit came back consistent in sign across every string — nut compensation negative on all six — which on its own could plausibly mean something real (a compensated nut sitting forward of the zero-fret line rather than behind it, exactly the geometry this app's own nut diagrams now draw). But the total compensation the fit wants (nut + saddle together) comes out roughly 7–10× smaller than the already-validated total every other method here agrees on, consistently across all six strings — not a re-apportioning of the same real number, an outright collapse of it. Tracing that down: 0.382in of saddle-only compensation, run through deltaCentsFromComp(), predicts about 52 cents of correction at fret 12; the raw per-fret error formula says only about 12–14 cents actually needs fixing there. That's roughly a 4× mismatch between the two halves of the model — not a sign or framing issue, a real scale mismatch between deltaCentsFromComp() (how many cents a given nut/saddle length shift produces at fret n) and computeFretErrorCents()/buildActionModel() (how many raw cents of error exist at fret n to begin with). Those two pieces were written separately and never jointly calibrated against a real reference.

To pick this back up productively, one of two things is needed: Gore's exact finer per-fret equations, correctly reconstructed and validated (the first two attempts above both failed independent checks on this specific piece); or real per-fret reference numbers — not just the nut/saddle totals Table 21-2 already gave us, but the actual per-fret compensation or tuning-error values from a real worked example — to calibrate deltaCentsFromComp() and the raw-error formula against each other directly. Until then, the Advanced option above stays on the data-calibrated split described earlier on this page; this third attempt is parked, not wired into any calculation. (Update, see the Optimized (Price) method's own "?" button: buildActionModel() and computeFretErrorCents() are now reused by that method, purely to measure raw per-fret error, not to drive a joint fit — deltaCentsFromComp(), the specific piece that was 4x off, is still unused and still parked.)

How the Optimized (Price) nut/saddle split works

This method is based on a self-published article, "Optimized Guitar Intonation" by Charlie Price, that you provided as photographed pages. Price's article makes one point the two methods above don't use: a fretted note's vibrating length runs from the fret to the saddle, so the nut's position has zero effect on any fretted note's pitch. That means the saddle can be positioned purely to get fretted notes in tune, without the nut in the picture at all — and only afterward does the nut get set, purely to fix the open string.

Saddle: minimizing total fretted-note error

For a candidate saddle setback, this app sums the absolute tuning error, in cents, at every fret from 1 up to your Number of Frets setting — absolute value so errors in opposite directions can't cancel each other out on paper, the same criterion Price's own article states directly. The raw per-fret error at each fret reuses this app's existing per-fret action model (buildActionModel() + computeFretErrorCents(), Gore's circle+ellipse+line neck-curvature construction, the same one Advanced mode's in-development third attempt above uses) purely as a per-fret error measurement — not run through deltaCentsFromComp(), the specific piece of that other attempt found to be roughly 4x off. Instead, how much a given saddle setback corrects each fret is computed the unambiguous way, directly from the definition of a cents shift: 1200·log₂(new vibrating length ÷ old vibrating length). Price's own article solves this with a general-purpose optimizer (Nelder-Mead simplex) and also lets fret placement itself move; this app only has saddle position as a free variable, and that total-error function is provably convex in saddle setback, so a golden-section search finds the same single best answer a general optimizer would.

Nut: Price's own published "optimized constant" preset

An earlier version of this section reasoned from first principles that nut compensation should equal the solved saddle setback (cancel the open string's added length by shortening it the same amount at the nut). That reasoning turned out to be wrong once the article's actual pages were available to read in full: Price's own Tables 3 and 4 show real nut compensation is not per-string and is not anywhere near the size of the saddle setback — it's one small, often-negative constant shared by the entire nut. His article gives two named variants: "fully optimized" nut compensation (each string carved individually) and "optimized constant" nut compensation (one shared value for the whole nut), and he states a clear preference for the latter in practice (his words: "I doubt that I will ever compensate the nut again using any other way than using the optimized constant nut compensation method"). His reason is that a single constant is what's actually practical to cut into a real nut, and the residual tuning error it leaves is small.

His article arrives at that constant by jointly re-optimizing fret placement, saddle position, and the nut constant together in one Nelder-Mead simplex run — which is why his real published fret positions differ slightly between the "fully optimized" and "optimized constant nut" scenarios in his Table 2. This app's frets are fixed at equal temperament, so it can't reproduce that joint optimization exactly. Rather than guess at a substitute formula, this app instead uses Price's own two published reference numbers directly as a preset, linearly interpolated/extrapolated by scale length:

24.9in scale → −0.0303in   25.4in scale → −0.0181in
(both for his reference string set: plain .012/.016, wound .024/.032/.042/.053, standard tuning)

That's what the Optimized (Price) method's Nut Comp. now shows: one shared value per string set, computed from those two points, not a per-string number and not equal to the saddle setback above.

What feeds this calculation

All four Advanced-mode inputs shown above the dropdown genuinely drive this one (unlike Advanced mode, where only Finger Depression currently moves the result): Nut Height and Neck Relief at fret 7 shape the per-fret action curve those errors are measured against, and Finger Depression plus Playing Style feed directly into each fret's raw error the same way they do in computeFretErrorCents() above.

A real bug caught and fixed during testing

A first version of this method used computeFretErrorCents() as-is, which deliberately omits bending stiffness — correctly, for the specific reason its own code comment gives (Gore's book found a fretting finger's effect on the neck-curvature model negligible). But that's a different physical effect from Simple mode's own already-validated comp = √(stretch² + stiffness²) formula, where "stiffness" is an inharmonicity-style effect from the string resisting a sharp bend at its fixed end points — present even on an open string, unrelated to finger pressure. Testing against a typical plain .012in high E string found that term is often the larger of the two, so leaving it out made this method's numbers come out several times too small — the exact same failure mode that got the in-development "third attempt" above parked. This version adds that same validated stiffness term back in at every fret before optimizing, and as a built-in self-check, its per-fret error at fret 12 alone now lands within a fraction of a percent of Simple mode's own number for the same string.

Honest limits

Finger-pressure is still modelled as a folded-in approximation rather than Price's or Gore's separately-measured geometry (see the comment above buildActionModel() in the code). This method also doesn't move fret placement the way Price's own article optimizes jointly — this app's frets stay equal-tempered — and, like Simple and Advanced mode, it computes each string's own compensation from that string's own scale length only, so in Multiscale mode it doesn't separately account for a fret's perpendicular-to-neck position the way the fanned-fret diagrams do. The nut number specifically is a straight-line interpolation between Price's own two published points and is calibrated to his reference light-gauge string set and standard tuning — treat it as a real, citable starting point rather than a from-scratch optimum for a very different gauge or tuning. This is the newest method in this app and the least externally cross-checked of the three — Simple mode is validated against a real independent calculator's output, Advanced mode against a real published worked example, and this one against the source article's own stated formulas/criterion for the saddle, the internal fret-12 self-check above, and the article's own Tables 3–4 numbers directly for the nut constant, not against a second independent reference. Worth spot-checking its numbers against Simple/Advanced on a representative string before trusting it for a real build.
